A nation of builders: American history for neighbors

This is not a lecture to give at the door. It is background, so you understand a long American habit: we build things — credit, factories, roads, farms, and schools — and we try to leave the next person a chance to rise. When a neighbor talks about jobs, costs, or whether anything still gets made here, this is part of the story. Listen first. Then, if it fits, connect the conversation to what is happening in our own towns.

Hamilton’s American System

In the first years of the United States, the country was new, in debt, and still buying many of the things it needed from abroad. Alexander Hamilton, the first Secretary of the Treasury, set out a plan in three reports to Congress.

On January 9, 1790, he submitted the Report on Public Credit. He argued that a nation has to pay what it owes, and keep its word, if it wants to borrow on fair terms and have a future. As he put it, “to be able to borrow upon good terms, it is essential that the credit of a nation should be well established.”

On December 13, 1790, he followed with the Report on a National Bank — a bank that could help the government manage its money and support trade and industry. Congress established that bank the following year.

On December 5, 1791, he submitted the Report on Manufactures. He asked how the United States could promote factories at home, “to render the United States, independent on foreign nations, for military and other essential supplies.” Protecting and building domestic industry, and backing the roads and other improvements a growing country would need, were part of the same idea: a nation that makes things, and can pay its way.

What later came to be called the American System grew out of this: national credit, protecting and building industry at home, and the infrastructure a growing country needs.

Lincoln and Americanism

Abraham Lincoln was a Whig before he was a Republican. He believed government should help people improve their condition — good roads, a chance to own land, a chance to learn a trade. In a campaign statement published March 15, 1832, he wrote that “the poorest and most thinly populated countries would be greatly benefitted by the opening of good roads, and in the clearing of navigable streams within their limits.” That is the language of internal improvements, a core piece of the American System, along with protective tariffs.

As president, in 1862, he signed three laws that put that idea to work:

  • The Homestead Act, May 20, 1862, gave settlers a chance to claim and improve a plot of public land.
  • The Pacific Railway Act, July 1, 1862, provided federal support to build a railroad and telegraph from the Missouri River to the Pacific.
  • The Morrill Land-Grant Act, July 2, 1862, granted public land to the states to found colleges of agriculture and the mechanic arts.

That is Americanism in practice: helping ordinary people build, farm, travel, and learn.

On September 30, 1859, speaking to the Wisconsin State Agricultural Society in Milwaukee, Lincoln described a free-labor system in which a “prudent, penniless beginner” can work, save, and then work on his own account. He called it “the just and generous, and prosperous system, which opens the way for all — gives hope to all, and energy, and progress, and improvement of condition to all.” Later writers have called that idea the right to rise. Those words are his.

And at Gettysburg, on November 19, 1863, he named the kind of government this country is supposed to be: “government of the people, by the people, for the people.”

Actions in that tradition

In our own time, President Trump has taken a set of documented steps that sit in the same tradition: protecting domestic industry, rewriting a continental trade agreement, and favoring American-made goods in government buying and in the marketplace. Here are the actions themselves, without a sales pitch.

In his first term, on March 8, 2018, he issued Proclamation 9705, imposing an additional 25 percent tariff on steel imports, and Proclamation 9704, imposing an additional 10 percent tariff on aluminum imports, under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act. Those duties took effect March 23, 2018. Beginning July 6, 2018, the United States also applied additional duties to certain imports from China under Section 301, after a finding concerning technology transfer, intellectual property, and innovation.

The United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement replaced NAFTA. Congress passed the Implementation Act, which became Public Law 116-113 on January 29, 2020, and the agreement entered into force on July 1, 2020.

On April 18, 2017, he signed Executive Order 13788, “Buy American and Hire American.” It directed the executive branch, consistent with law, to maximize the use of goods, products, and materials produced in the United States in federal procurement and federal financial assistance.

In his second term, on February 10, 2025, he issued Proclamations 10896 and 10895, applying a 25 percent tariff to steel and aluminum imports from all countries. On June 3, 2025, a further proclamation increased those steel and aluminum tariffs from 25 percent to 50 percent, effective June 4, 2025, with a different rate provided for the United Kingdom under a separate arrangement.

On April 2, 2025, Executive Order 14257 imposed additional “reciprocal” tariffs on imports, starting at 10 percent, with higher country-specific rates listed in an annex to the order. Those rates have since been modified by later orders. On March 13, 2026, Executive Order 14392 directed the Federal Trade Commission to prioritize enforcement against false “Made in America” claims, and called for review of American-origin claims on goods sold to the government.

What this means at the door

Do not lecture a neighbor about Hamilton or Lincoln. Do not turn a porch talk into a history class or a campaign speech. This page is for you, not for them.

What you hold is simple: this country has a long habit of building — credit, industry, roads, farms, schools — and of trying to leave ordinary people a way to rise. When someone talks about jobs leaving town, or grocery bills, or whether anything is made here anymore, listen first. Ask what they have seen in their own neighborhood. Then, if it fits, bridge to a practical local result: work, costs, and building things in our own towns.

Listen first. Talk from the heart. One person at a time.

We are the they.
