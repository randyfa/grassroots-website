Resentments: why people feel the way they do

This is not a topic to bring up at the door. It is background so you can understand the people you are talking to — that resentment is often behind the misperceptions and the “I’m done” feeling you will hear, that those people have been harmed by it, and that our job is to have empathy and then bridge appropriately.

Why this matters

A lot of the unaffiliated and soft Republican voters we meet are carrying real frustration toward politicians, parties, the media, and government in general. Those feelings keep them from re-engaging. They are not just “checked out.” Often it is fear and resentment.

In the training video, Randy calls this the key. For many people, the anger started with something that felt unfair or broken. They held on to it. It got fed — by the news, by what they see online, by another disappointment — and it grew. It can make them pull back from community, from family, and from voting.

As Randy says, “These feelings are real to them, and we have to acknowledge that and deal with it.” When they feel heard, doors start to open.

What it does to a person

You do not say any of this to a neighbor. You hold it so you can show up with compassion instead of an argument.

Randy puts it this way, from his 12-step work: resentment is often called drinking poison and waiting for the other person to die. The anger harms the person holding on to it more than the target. “I’ve come to think of it, it’s a cancer on the soul.”

It starts as anger. Then we hold on to it. The more it gets fed, the more it grows, until it can start to control how we see everything. It stresses the body. It locks us into an old injury, and the story repeats. It narrows our perception. The people we are angry at start to look like a cartoon, not a neighbor.

If we come at someone hard in that state, that is all they will hear. They will not be able to hear what we have to say.

Look at our own side first

Before we walk up to a door, we have to look at our own resentments too. Randy uses the Pigpen image from the Peanuts cartoon: an angry cloud around a person. We are affecting the people around us.

“We have to look at our cloud, our resentments. We have to lower that and come at them from heart to heart.”

We reap what we sow. Our own negative comments and put-down posts add to that cloud. If we want to help change other people, we have to be willing to change ourselves.

What this means at the door

Do not bring up resentment. Do not diagnose it. Do not tell someone they are bitter, brainwashed, or that they have a “mind virus.” As Randy says, “We do not diagnose. Okay? We just seek to understand.” The moment we use those labels, the walls come up.

What you do instead:

  • Stay calm. Breathe. Be curious. Don’t let your own frustration drive the conversation.
  • Invite what’s most frustrating, and reflect it back: “What I’m hearing you say is this.”
  • Don’t correct or debate. Compassion is not the same as agreement.
  • Bridge only after they feel heard — to a practical local issue, and to a reason to stay engaged.
  • If the conversation is hostile, thank them and move on. Protect your own peace. One closed door does not define the day.

Here is some of the language Randy already uses. This is listening and bridging, not a lecture on resentment:

“A lot of people tell me they’re frustrated or let down. So, what’s been the biggest thing for you?”

“Yeah, that makes sense. I felt that way also. And a lot of good people have.”

If they’re not open: “Thanks for being honest with me. If you ever want to talk again, just let me know.”

Our job is not to fix them. We’re not a therapist. We listen first, talk from the heart, and leave the door open.

Listen first. Talk from the heart. One person at a time.

We are the they.
