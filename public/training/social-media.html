Online social media manipulation: how perceptions get shaped

This is not a topic to bring up at the door. You do not need to explain algorithms, bots, or deepfakes to a neighbor. You just need to understand that these things help shape the misperceptions you will hear, that people have been harmed by them, have empathy for them, and then bridge appropriately — listen first, and keep it to what’s affecting their family.

How the feed works

Facebook, YouTube, TikTok, and X earn money by keeping people scrolling. Anger and fear hold attention best, so each feed gradually fills with more of what upsets the person, plus posts that confirm what they already believe.

That is not an accident, and it is not the neighbor’s fault. The feed is built that way. Over months and years, someone can get a distorted picture of their own town without ever choosing it.

Cable news can do a similar thing in a living room. Randy talks about door-knocking and seeing older people who sit home watching one news channel all day: “No wonder they’re upset. They’re not hearing the truth. They’re getting one side of the message.” The same cycle shows up on a phone. The more someone watches or taps, the more the system shows them of the same thing. Views get entrenched.

As Randy says, “One conversation after another will overcome these deeply entrenched views and years of online immersion.”

Memes, bots, and influencers

Randy names some of what fuels today’s culture of offendedness and division: mainstream media, false news, online memes, bots, hateful messaging, and influencers. In plain words:

  • A meme can travel faster than a fact, and the put-down ones add to the resentment culture. Randy has had to stop posting those himself. “I’m not going to win an argument online.”
  • Bots are fake or automated accounts that can make a message look more popular, more angry, or more widely shared than it really is.
  • Influencers — on YouTube, TikTok, Facebook, X, and elsewhere — can shape what people believe because the relationship feels personal, even when the incentive is still attention.

You do not need to explain any of that at the door. Just know it may be in the background of what you are hearing.

Deepfakes and AI-generated content

A deepfake is a video, photo, or audio clip made or altered with software so it looks or sounds like a real person said or did something they did not. AI tools can also write posts, fake headlines, and images that look like news.

People can be taken in by this without meaning to. That is part of how perceptions get shaped, and another reason to have empathy instead of an argument. You still do not raise it at the door. If something sounds off, you do not have to debate whether a clip is real. Ask what they have seen in their own neighborhood.

What this means at the door

Do not bring up social media, algorithms, bots, or deepfakes. Do not tell someone they have been manipulated, lied to, or brainwashed. The walls will come up.

What you do instead:

  • Recognize it quietly to yourself: “that’s the feed talking.” You keep that thought to yourself. You do not say it out loud.
  • Don’t argue with it. You’re not going to win an argument online, and you won’t win it by fighting the feed on a porch either.
  • Ask what they have seen in their own neighborhood — grocery bills, safety, schools, water. A real neighbor conversation is the one thing the algorithm can’t provide.
  • Listen first. Talk from the heart. Bridge to a practical local issue.

This is the other side of “Return to the original social media — neighbors talking to neighbors.” The feed keeps people isolated and certain. A short, face-to-face talk can cut through, one issue at a time, one person at a time.

Listen first. Talk from the heart. One person at a time.

We are the they.
