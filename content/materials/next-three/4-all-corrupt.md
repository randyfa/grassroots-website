## Short Bridge
*“Some of that feeling is fair. Local turnout still decides who we get.”*

## The Issue and Expanded Bridge
**The issue**
The belief that “they’re all corrupt” or “nothing ever changes” is a common expression of frustration. It often mixes real examples of misconduct with a broader sense that participation is pointless.
**Short background**
Some skepticism is justified. At the same time, local offices and local races are decided by turnout. When fewer people participate, the same small groups continue to set policy on taxes, growth, water, safety, and schools. Local results can and do change when neighbors organize around concrete issues.
**The bridge**
“Some of that feeling is fair. Local turnout still decides who we get. On the issue that matters most to your family right now, would you be open to one practical step so the people who show up include you?” Listen, mirror, and close on a single commitment.
*Ownership close: “We are the they. What’s the one block or one person you’ll cover?”*

## Deeper Background
**Why the claim resonates**
High-profile national and state examples of misconduct or unfulfilled promises reinforce the sense that systems are rigged or inert. The generalization (“they’re all the same / nothing changes”) becomes a protective stance that reduces the emotional cost of disengagement.
**Supporting context**

* Local elections in Yavapai County (school boards, municipal races, bonds, county offices) are frequently decided by relatively small margins or low absolute numbers of voters.
* Policy on growth management, water, public safety, and local taxes is set by the officials who win those races.
* Organized neighbor-level participation (registration, one block covered, PC interest) has historically shifted local outcomes more reliably than national messaging.
* Acknowledging that some skepticism is fair lowers defensiveness and opens the door to a practical local discussion.

**The perspective shift**
The useful shift is from “nothing changes so I won’t participate” to “local turnout still decides who sets the rules on the issue I care about.” Corruption or inertia at higher levels does not erase the effect of local participation. The person does not need to trust the system wholesale; they only need to influence one local result. “We are the they” is the ownership response to the claim that nothing changes.
**Ownership close**
“We are the they. What’s the one thing you’ll own?”

## 2-Minute Two-Person Conversation
*Setting: Front porch or sidewalk. Neighbor has just said they’re all corrupt and nothing changes.*

**Neighbor:** They’re all corrupt. Nothing ever changes.  
**You:** Some of that feeling is fair. We’ve all seen examples that justify the frustration.  
**Neighbor:** So why bother?  
**You:** Local turnout still decides who we get. The people who show up still decide taxes, growth, water, safety, and schools. Which of those is hitting your family most?  
**Neighbor:** Taxes, for sure. And the growth.  
**You:** That’s decided right here. On that issue, would you be open to one practical step so the people who show up include you — checking your registration, or talking with one neighbor?  
**Neighbor:** I guess I could talk to my neighbor.  
**You:** That’s great. I’ll follow up next week. We are the they.

**Video script (one speaker, about 2 minutes)**
When someone says, “They’re all corrupt — nothing ever changes,” part of that feeling is fair. We’ve all seen examples that justify the frustration.
The bridge is straightforward: “Some of that feeling is fair. Local turnout still decides who we get.”
Then we bring it local. The people who show up still decide taxes, growth, water, safety, and schools. We ask which of those is hitting their family most, we listen, and we offer one practical step so the people who show up include them.
That’s the shift: from “nothing changes” to “local turnout still decides this, and I can be part of who shows up.”
Neighbors talking to neighbors. We are the they.
