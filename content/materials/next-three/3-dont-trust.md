## Short Bridge
*“I hear the distrust. On the things that protect your family locally—costs, safety, schools—which still matters most to you?”*

## The Issue and Expanded Bridge
**The issue**
Distrust of both parties is common among independents and disengaged Republicans. It usually comes from years of national promises that did not match results, or from the sense that both sides prioritize messaging over delivery.
**Short background**
Trust is rebuilt more easily on local, concrete issues than on national identity. Costs, neighborhood safety, water with growth, school options, and infrastructure are problems residents experience directly. Local decisions on those issues are still made by people who show up. Starting with the person’s own concern rather than a party label reduces the defensive reaction.
**The bridge**
“Trust is earned. That’s why I start with what’s hitting your family, not a party speech. What’s the biggest thing affecting you and your family right now — costs, safety, water, schools?” Listen, mirror, and connect to one practical local step.
*Ownership close when ready: “We are the they. What’s the one block or one person you’ll cover?”*

## Deeper Background
**Why distrust is widespread**
National polarization and repeated cycles of high rhetoric followed by limited delivery have eroded trust across the spectrum. Many voters no longer grant either party the benefit of the doubt. The emotional starting point is skepticism, not curiosity.
**Supporting context**

* Local issues (costs, housing, water constraints with growth, public safety, school quality) are experienced directly and can be discussed without requiring party identification.
* Trust is more readily rebuilt through consistent local follow-through than through national messaging.
* Neighbor-to-neighbor conversations that begin with the other person’s stated concern lower defensiveness and create space for a practical next step.
* In Yavapai County, growth-related pressures on water, housing, and infrastructure remain frequent resident concerns that are decided at the local level.

**The perspective shift**
Distrust does not have to be resolved before action. The useful shift is from “I don’t trust them” to “I can still influence the one local result that affects my family.” Starting with the person’s own concern earns the right to continue the conversation. No party loyalty is required. Trust is earned one conversation at a time.
**Ownership close**
“We are the they. What’s the one thing you’ll own?”

## 2-Minute Two-Person Conversation
*Setting: Front porch or sidewalk. Neighbor has just said they don’t trust either side.*

**Neighbor:** Honestly, I don’t trust either side.  
**You:** I hear you. A lot of people have been disappointed before.  
**Neighbor:** They all promise and nothing happens.  
**You:** Trust is earned. That’s why I start with what’s hitting your family, not a party speech. What’s the biggest thing affecting you and your family right now — costs, safety, water, schools?  
**Neighbor:** Costs. And the water, with all the building going on.  
**You:** Those get decided locally, by the people who show up. You don’t have to trust anybody to have a say in that. Would you be open to one practical step — checking your registration, or talking with one neighbor about the water?  
**Neighbor:** Maybe. I could check my registration.  
**You:** That’s great. I’ll follow up next week. We are the they.

**Video script (one speaker, about 2 minutes)**
When someone says, “I don’t trust either side,” they’re usually telling you they’ve been disappointed before.
The bridge we use is simple: “Trust is earned. That’s why I start with what’s hitting your family, not a party speech.”
Then we ask what’s affecting them most right now — costs, safety, water with the growth, schools. We listen. We mirror. We connect it to one practical local step they can take.
That’s how trust starts to rebuild: not with a speech, but with a neighbor conversation about a real local issue and one thing they can do about it.
Neighbors talking to neighbors. We are the they.
